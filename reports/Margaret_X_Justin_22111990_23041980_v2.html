Why You Met

The two of you didn't meet by accident, and you weren't brought together to coast. You're here to help each other grow inward—not the kind of growth that gets applauded at dinner parties, but the real thing: self-knowledge, patience, the slow work of becoming wiser and less afraid. This pairing carries the Hermit, and that means you're being asked to go deeper than surface companionship. You're meant to teach each other how to be still, how to respect what's unfinished, how to hold space for the parts of yourselves that don't perform well.

When it's working, this relationship becomes a refuge. You give each other permission to retreat, to think, to be quiet without needing to explain. There's a respect for solitude here that most couples never learn. You can sit in the same room and do entirely separate things and feel closer for it. The Hermit doesn't rush. It doesn't demand constant proof of affection. It trusts that depth takes time, and you're both willing to let that time pass without panic.

But when it tips, Margaret, Justin—one or both of you can pull so far inward that the other person starts to feel like a guest in their own relationship. The need for space becomes avoidance. The respect for privacy becomes emotional lockdown. You stop talking about what's actually happening inside you, and suddenly you're two people living alone together, each waiting for the other to surface. That loneliness inside intimacy is the shadow here, and it's worse than being on your own because at least then you're not pretending.

So the work is this: don't let solitude harden into silence. Talk, even when it's awkward. Make time to be together with intention, not just proximity. If you're going inward, bring each other along sometimes. Spiritual practice, real conversation, anything that lets you witness each other's inner life—that's what keeps the Hermit from becoming isolation. You met to grow. Make sure you're doing it side by side.

What Goes Unsaid

The eight sits here, and it names the silence that kills. One of you—or both in turn—will begin to feel that this relationship is unfair. Not dramatic, not loud. Just the slow accretion of evidence that someone is not holding up their end. That one voice gets heard and the other doesn't. That you're giving more than you're getting, or that Justin decides and Margaret adjusts, or the reverse. The math doesn't add up, and neither of you says it out loud.

This is what festers: the unspoken complaint. The swallowed objection. The moment you let something go because bringing it up feels too hard, too small, too late. You tell yourself it doesn't matter. It matters. The eight doesn't care about good intentions or how much you love each other—it measures weight, and when the scales tip, resentment moves in.

The break, if it comes, will not be because you fought. It will be because one of you finally admits—maybe only to yourself—that you have not been heard. That your needs have been background noise. When you can't find a fair solution to the questions that actually matter, Margaret, when the same imbalance shows up again and again and there's no willingness to see it, the bond snaps.

So the work here is to say the thing before it becomes the reason. To name unfairness when it's still small. To ask for the rebalancing before you've written the story of why it can't happen. The eight will not let you skip this. Silence here is rot, and honesty—specific, uncomfortable, now—is the only thing that holds.
